# Speaker notes — KD PAY × Airwallex (white-label proposal)

Compiled from the notes pane of `KD-PAY-Airwallex-White-Label-Proposal.pptx`. The same text is embedded
in the deck, so Presenter View shows it while you speak. Timings assume a 25-minute slot.

---

## Slide 01 — White-label payments for Asia, on Airwallex rails

Opening (45 seconds). We want to run the merchant-facing layer in Asia on your rails — our brand, our merchants, our merchant-level risk; your licence, your acquiring, your settlement. The deck is about the business model, not the software: who does what, how the money works, and what we need from you to start.

## Slide 02 — You own the rails. We own the merchant.

This is the whole idea on one page. Airwallex keeps every regulated function — you stay the licensed party, you keep settlement, you keep the network relationships. We take the customer-facing side: brand, sales, onboarding ops, support, disputes, reserves. The merchant never has to deal with two companies. If they push: we are not trying to be a processor, we are trying to be your distribution channel in Asia.

## Slide 03 — Three things, and what you get back

Say these three out loud, in this order. Programme approval is the gate — without it nothing else matters. The corridor list is what decides our roadmap; we will not promise a market we cannot service. Commercials can be fixed after the model is agreed, but the reserve terms should reflect the 0.01% record from day one.

## Slide 04 — Who we are

Be upfront about the entity change before they ask: the team and the book are from 2022, the LLC is new. Underwriting will find it, so we frame it first — and slide 17 offers statements and references under NDA. The 'model sought' line is deliberate: we are applying for a white-label programme, we are not claiming we already have one.

## Slide 05 — Asian merchants can sell globally. They cannot get paid globally.

This is the business case, not a product pitch. Every point is something we hear from merchants weekly. Avoid quoting decline percentages unless we have our own data — 'materially higher' is defensible, an invented number is not.

## Slide 06 — Asia first, then the world

Ask them directly: which of these corridors can you support today, and which are 6-12 months away? That answer re-orders our roadmap. We deliberately do not promise all of Asia on day one.

## Slide 07 — Four lines of revenue, one merchant relationship

They will want to know how we make money, because it tells them whether we will survive. Four lines, all volume-linked. Be explicit that we do not touch float: merchant funds sit in your ledger, not in our bank account. That single sentence removes most of the risk questions about a small partner.

## Slide 08 — The merchant sees one company. You stay the rail.

This slide exists to kill the two objections that come with the word white-label: 'are you hiding us from the merchant' and 'are you asking to use our licence'. The answer is no to both. Your name stays in the merchant agreement and on the statement descriptor where regulation requires it — that is what a defensible white-label programme looks like, and it is what we want to run.

## Slide 09 — Three ways to sign this — and why we recommend the first

Do not oversell A. Say: we think A is fastest for both of us, but B is a perfectly good starting point if your risk committee needs to hold the record — we can migrate to A later without re-papering merchants. C is the model you already run for marketplaces; we would use it only for the handful of enterprise merchants who need their own account and are happy to be KYC'd twice.

## Slide 10 — How merchants actually arrive

The user has asked to keep software out of this deck, so keep this high level: three ways in, no architecture. The point for Airwallex is distribution — we already sit next to these merchants. If they ask what is built today, be precise: the integration layer is in build, the merchant base and the operations are live.

## Slide 11 — What the model is worth at our volume

Be careful here: these are our take-rate scenarios, not their pricing. The purpose is to show the shape of the business — revenue scales linearly with volume, and our cost to serve is largely fixed. If they ask for a real model, say we will build it with their pricing sheet within a week. That is a much stronger answer than an invented spreadsheet.

## Slide 12 — Our dispute record, against the threshold that matters

Say the 0.9% figure as 'around' — Visa and Mastercard use different programmes and bands. The point is the ratio, not the exact line. 90x headroom is arithmetic: 0.9 divided by 0.01. Then move straight to reserves: offering to fund them ourselves is what makes a small partner safe to board.

## Slide 13 — Eight commitments that protect your rails

Keep this at the business level: they are checking whether a small partner is a liability on their rails. The strongest sentence is the last one — an honest scope statement beats an inflated claim, and it invites them to tell us which controls their own risk team needs to see. Offer the security architecture document as follow-up rather than walking through it here.

## Slide 14 — Why us, and why now

Careful with the comparison — say it as a coverage observation, not a criticism of competitors. The honest answer to 'what would make you leave' is: coverage gaps in South Asia, and pricing that does not reflect our dispute record.

## Slide 15 — Where this goes if it works

Be careful not to inflate. The $2M is our ambition, not a forecast — say so. If they ask for a numbered forecast, offer to model it with their pricing sheet and send it within a week; that is a stronger answer than an invented spreadsheet.

## Slide 16 — Four decisions to make this week

Close by asking for the two things that unblock everything: written confirmation of the structure, and sandbox access. Replace the bracketed contact details before sending — they are intentionally left as placeholders.

## Slide 17 — What we can provide during due diligence

Ending on the entity issue is deliberate: it is the one thing underwriting will find, so we frame it before they do. Say we would rather over-share than have it surface late in the process.
